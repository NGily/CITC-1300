Getting the facts straight

Although Geckos are known for their many unique traits, throughout the ages there are some interesting interpretations of what geckos can do in many different cultures.

Gasp! Danger!

Found in: Parts of Africa, the Caribbean, and rural areas in India and Latin America Belief: If a gecko falls on your skin—especially your face or open wounds—you’ll get sick, be cursed, or even die. Origins: This likely stems from fear of small, fast creatures that appear at night. Because geckos cling to walls and ceilings, people may think they are unnatural or magical. Truth: Geckos are harmless. They do not have venom or dangerous bacteria. If anything, they help humans by eating disease-carrying insects like mosquitoes.

Worse than seeing a black cat?

Good luck: Thailand, Indonesia, Polynesia Bad luck: Some African and Caribbean traditions Belief: In Thailand and Bali, a gecko chirping near you is a sign that you are on the right path or something good will happen. In some West African or Caribbean cultures, geckos are associated with spirits or omens—good or bad depending on the context. Truth: Geckos do make chirping sounds, especially at night. They are territorial calls or mating signals. The associations with luck are symbolic—like a spiritual interpretation of nature.

Gastly singing!

Found in: India, Indonesia, the Philippines Belief: If a gecko makes a noise while someone speaks, it confirms what they say is true—or ominously false. In some areas, gecko chirps at certain times of day or night are said to foretell death, marriage, or a visitor. Origins: Gecko vocalizations are loud and sudden, which people interpreted as “mystical interruptions.” Oral traditions linked natural sounds to fate or divine commentary. Truth: The gecko’s call is just part of its normal behavior. No evidence connects chirps to fate or destiny.

Spitting Venom!

Found in: Rural Southeast Asia, parts of Central America Belief: Geckos can spit corrosive or infectious fluid at enemies. Origins: Possibly a confusion with spitting cobras or venomous lizards like the Gila monster or beaded lizard, which are not geckos. Fear of creatures that can climb and hide may have added to the mystery. Truth: Geckos don’t spit anything. They don’t even have venom glands. If threatened, they may drop their tails or scurry away—but never attack.

Guides from the great beyond?

Found in: Middle East, Pacific Islands, tribal African religions Belief: A gecko entering your home or showing up unexpectedly may signal a visitor, spirit, or important event. In some traditional beliefs, the gecko is a messenger between worlds. Origins: In areas where traditional beliefs include animal symbolism, geckos are seen as spiritual intermediaries. Their sudden, silent appearances seem mysterious—perfect for myth-making. Truth: Geckos enter homes because of food (insects) and warmth.

Why Dose It Matter?

Understanding gecko myths gives us insight into: Cultural interpretations of nature. How fear and wonder shape folklore. Why some animals are protected in one culture and feared in another. They also remind us that the same animal can be a guardian, curse, or curiosity, depending on the stories we tell.
